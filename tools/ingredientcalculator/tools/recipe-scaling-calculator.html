How scaling actually behaves

Flour, sugar, milk and water, and the fats like butter and oil all scale one to one with your factor. Multiply and move on.

Baking powder, baking soda, and yeast are the trap. Push them up at full strength and a large batch can dome, then sink, or turn soapy. One common fix scales them by the square root of the conversion factor to hold back the rise. That square root move is a baker's rule of thumb, not a chemistry law, and it still shifts with altitude and humidity. Another common guideline just trims leavening back once you reach a double batch or more.

Salt behaves the same way. Bring it below the straight line amount first, then taste and raise it. Strong, hot spices like cayenne and dried chili start lighter still and get adjusted once the batch is together.

Bake time does not ride along with size. A smaller batch can be done well before you expect, so read it by doneness, not by the clock times your factor. For a deeper or fuller pan, drop the oven a little and give it more time.

Where these numbers come from

Every gram here starts on a published chart, then gets a re-weigh on a calibrated scale in our own kitchen. We transcribe a density once, stamp the version, and let the converter compute grams as cups times that density in code. We do not type a weight by hand. We have set a spoon and level cup against a dip and pack cup ourselves, and watched one cup of flour swing by a wide margin on the same scale. Where two charts round a little differently, we show the weight home bakers meet most often and say where it came from.

The salt factor (0.75) follows the common baking guideline of trimming salt back for larger batches. The spice factor (0.65) is our own editorial starting point for strong, hot spices like cayenne, chili, and cloves. It sits below the salt line because heat compounds in a big pot. Neither number is a chemistry law. Both are starting points. Taste the batch and adjust.

Common questions about scaling a recipe

How do I scale a recipe up or down
Divide what you want by what the recipe makes, then multiply each ingredient by that factor. Flour, sugar, and liquids go straight up, so a calculator does the work faster than converting fractions by hand.
Why can I not just double the baking powder
Chemical leavening scales below the line. Doubled at full strength it can over rise and then collapse, so many bakers scale it by the square root of the factor instead.
How much salt when I make a bigger batch
Start a little under the straight line amount, then taste and bring it up. Salt reads stronger in a big pot than a plain multiply suggests.
Can I swap a large rectangle pan for two round pans
Compare them by area. A rectangle is its length times its width, a round is its radius times itself times pi, and when those square inches line up the swap holds. The pan tool above does the arithmetic.
How do I split half an egg
Crack and whisk a whole egg, weigh it, and use half the weight. A large egg out of the shell has a steady weight, which beats guessing by eye.
