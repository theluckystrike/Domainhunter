The art and science of recipe scaling

Doubling a recipe is rarely as simple as doubling every number.

Scaling a recipe sounds simple: to double the servings, double everything. In practice it is more nuanced. Some ingredients scale linearly, others do not, and the cooking process itself changes when you work with larger or smaller volumes. This guide covers what you need to scale recipes confidently, and the calculator above applies these rules automatically.

When scaling works perfectly

Most core ingredients scale proportionally. If a recipe for 4 servings calls for 2 cups of flour, a recipe for 8 needs 4 cups. This holds for flour, sugar, butter, eggs at moderate scales, meats, vegetables, grains, pasta, and dairy. For scaling up to about 3x or down to 1/2x, you can safely multiply every ingredient by the factor and expect good results. Liquids in soups, stews, and braises scale linearly too — the ratio of liquid to solid stays the same, it just happens in a bigger pot.

Ingredients that do not scale linearly

This is where most home cooks run into trouble. Several categories behave differently at different scales:

  • Baking powder and baking soda: when doubling, use about 1.5x to 1.75x rather than a full 2x. Too much leavening makes baked goods rise fast then collapse into a dense, sunken result.
  • Yeast: increase by about 1.5x when doubling and 2x when tripling. Yeast reproduces during proofing, so give it time rather than a lot more yeast; excess produces off-flavors and a coarse crumb.
  • Salt: start at 1.5x when doubling, then taste. Saltiness is not perceived linearly, and over-salting is hard to fix — you can add more but not take it away.
  • Strong spices and extracts: cayenne, cinnamon, cloves, and vanilla should scale conservatively at about 1.5x when doubling, then taste-test.
  • Garlic and onion: aromatics can overpower when scaled aggressively. Use about 1.75x for doubling and 2.5x for tripling.

The scaler flags these ingredients and applies a gentler multiplier, then leaves a note so you can finish to taste.

Cooking time and temperature

Cooking time does not scale proportionally. Doubling a recipe does not double the time. A larger volume takes longer to reach internal temperature, but the surface cooks at the same rate. On the stovetop, the main change is the time to reach a simmer. For baking, a doubled batch split across two standard pans bakes in roughly the original time; the same batter in one larger pan needs 10 to 25 percent more time, and often 25 degrees Fahrenheit lower to keep the outside from over-browning. For roasts, weight and thickness drive the time, so use an instant-read thermometer rather than the clock.

Scaling down: half and quarter batches

Scaling down brings its own challenges. Eggs are the classic problem: to halve a recipe with one egg, beat the whole egg and use half, about 1.5 tablespoons. Tiny amounts like 1/8 teaspoon of baking soda are hard to measure accurately, so consider mini measuring spoons or a scale that reads to 0.1 grams. Pan size matters too — a halved batter in the original pan will be shallow and bake faster, so choose a smaller pan for the same depth.

Frequently asked questions

Can I simply double all ingredients when doubling a recipe?

For core ingredients like flour, sugar, butter, meat, and vegetables, yes. But leavening agents, salt, and strong spices should be scaled more conservatively — about 1.5x to 1.75x when doubling. The scaler flags these ingredients with notes so you can add the rest to taste.

Does cooking time change when I scale a recipe?

It changes, but not proportionally. Doubling a stovetop recipe mainly adds time to reach temperature. Baking in same-size pans keeps the time roughly equal; a larger pan needs 10 to 25 percent more time and often 25°F lower. Always confirm with a thermometer.

How do I halve a recipe that calls for 1 egg?

Beat the whole egg and measure out about 1.5 tablespoons (half of one large egg). For cakes where the egg gives structure, use one yolk instead; for binders like meatballs, a whole egg usually works fine even in the halved recipe.

What pan size should I use for a scaled recipe?

Choose a pan that keeps roughly the same batter depth as the original. Doubling a 9-inch round? Use two of them or one 13x9-inch pan. Halving? Try a 6-inch round or an 8x4-inch loaf pan. Depth affects both baking time and texture.

Can I scale a recipe for a slow cooker or Instant Pot?

Scale the ingredients but never exceed the max fill line — two-thirds for pressure cookers, three-quarters for slow cookers. Pressure-cooker times stay the same; slow-cooker times may need a modest increase, and liquid often does not need to grow as much due to less evaporation.
