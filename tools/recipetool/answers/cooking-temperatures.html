The science of cooking temperatures

Temperature, not the clock, is what actually tells you food is done.

Hitting the right internal temperature at the right moment determines the texture, juiciness, and safety of everything you cook. It is the single skill that most reliably separates mediocre results from excellent ones — more than any recipe, pan, or technique.

Why internal temperature beats time

"Cook for 20 minutes" is inherently imprecise. Thickness, starting temperature, and ovens that run 25-50°F off their dial all change how long food takes. The only dependable indicator is internal temperature read with an accurate instant-read thermometer in the thickest part. A steak is medium-rare at 130-135°F whether it took 8 minutes or 12.

USDA guidelines vs. culinary preference

The USDA sets minimum safe temperatures with a wide margin: 145°F with a 3-minute rest for whole cuts of beef, pork and lamb; 160°F for ground meats; 165°F for all poultry. But safety is a function of temperature and time — holding chicken at 150°F for about 2.8 minutes is as safe as an instant 165°F, which is why sous vide can produce juicier, safe chicken.

Carryover cooking: the hidden rise

Meat keeps cooking after it leaves the heat because the hot exterior continues warming the center. A large prime rib can rise 10-15°F; a thin chop only 3-5°F. Pull standard cuts 5-10°F below target and large roasts 10-15°F below, then rest — tented loosely with foil — about 5 minutes per inch of thickness so juices redistribute.

Altitude: the overlooked variable

Lower pressure at elevation drops water's boiling point about 2°F per 1,000 feet (roughly 202°F at 5,000 feet), so boiling and braising take longer. Baking is affected even more: leavened goods rise faster and can collapse. Typical fixes are raising oven temperature 15-25°F, trimming sugar, adding 2-4 tablespoons of liquid per cup, and cutting leavening slightly. The calculator above estimates these for your elevation.

Choosing a thermometer

An instant-read digital thermometer accurate to about 1°F and reading in 2-3 seconds is the gold standard. Insert it into the thickest part, avoiding bone, fat, and gristle, which conduct heat differently. For roasting and smoking, a leave-in probe with an external display lets you monitor without opening the oven, which can drop its temperature 25-50°F each time.

Frequently asked questions

What temperature kills bacteria in meat?

Most harmful bacteria die at 165°F (74°C) instantly. Lower temperatures held longer achieve the same result — chicken at 150°F for 2.8 minutes is equally safe. Whole cuts of beef and pork are safe at 145°F with a 3-minute rest, since bacteria live only on the surface.

Why does the USDA recommend different temperatures for different meats?

Different meats carry different risks. Whole cuts of beef and pork have bacteria only on the surface (safe at 145°F + rest). Grinding mixes surface bacteria throughout, so ground meat needs 160°F. Poultry can carry Salmonella within the muscle, so it needs 165°F. Fish is safe at 145°F.

How does altitude affect cooking and baking?

Lower pressure means water boils cooler (about 2°F less per 1,000 feet), so boiling and braising take longer. For baking, increase oven temp 15-25°F, reduce sugar, add liquid, and reduce leavening. Use the altitude calculator above for figures tuned to your elevation.

What is carryover cooking and how much rise should I expect?

It is the continued temperature rise after removing food from heat. Large roasts rise 10-15°F, steaks and chops 3-5°F, and thin fish fillets 1-2°F. Pull meat 5-15°F below your target and rest it tented with foil so it finishes at the right temperature.

What is the best thermometer for checking meat temperature?

An instant-read digital thermometer (reads in 2-3 seconds, accurate to about 1°F) is the most versatile. Insert it into the thickest part, avoiding bone. For roasting, use a leave-in probe with an external display. Avoid slow, less accurate dial thermometers.
